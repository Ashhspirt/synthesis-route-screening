Route searches are expensive. Start with the molecules most likely to succeed.

A small neural ensemble that runs on a CPU. From molecular structure alone, it predicts whether a historical AiZynthFinder search found a synthesis route, so expensive searches can go to the most promising candidates first.

From SMILES to a screening decision

The whole pipeline runs on two CPU threads with no GPU. A single prediction only loads the three frozen models.

Parse the structure

The SMILES string is read in. Invalid input raises an explicit error instead of being skipped.

Encode features

A Morgan count fingerprint (radius 2, 1,024 bits) passed through log1p, plus five descriptors scaled on training data only: 1,029 inputs.

Score with three networks

Hidden layers 256 → 128 → 64, ReLU, dropout 0.2. One model per seed (42, 43, 44), each restored at its best validation loss.

Average and decide

The three sigmoid outputs are averaged; a mean of 0.5844 or more is prioritised. The threshold was chosen on validation data to favour fewer missed candidates.

Know its blind spots

The more complex the structure, the more often a molecule with a route gets deferred. Below are false-negative rates by structural subgroup at the recall-oriented threshold.

False-negative rate: complex structures vs the rest

Orange is the complex group; grey is all other molecules.

The score describes whether AiZynthFinder historically found a route. It gives no reaction route, conditions or yields; assessing a synthesis still needs a chemist.

For molecules with more than four rings, the false-negative rate is 26.51%, about four times that of the rest. Avoid deferring ring-rich candidates on this score alone.

The official test set has already been evaluated once. Further method development needs newly reserved or external evaluation data.
